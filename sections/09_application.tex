\section{Applications as Identity-Criterion Tests}
\label{sec:application}
% =============================================================================

Sections~\ref{sec:inventory}--\ref{sec:determinacy} define a selected
inventory, candidate carrier--basis profiles, and their stipulated
transformation classifications.
The following settings illustrate questions
that those distinctions can help formulate.

For each setting, the relevant questions are which referent is intended,
which presentations are eligible for comparison, what changes under a
transformation, and what evidence justifies sameness or difference.
Where a common referential judgment cannot be warranted, the Neutral
Substrate construction does not license adopting it as an unqualified
foundational commitment merely because the records share a label.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Multi-Jurisdiction Regulatory Reporting}
\label{subsec:app_regulatory}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

A regulatory record may refer to a corporation, a subsidiary, an office,
or a party occupying an office.
The selected $\OBL$ profile offers a
candidate basis for following an obligation-bearer across changes in
reported duties and activities.
Whether the same legal or institutional
bearer persists requires an appropriate continuity criterion; sharing a
name, role, or identifier is not sufficient.
Different jurisdictions may
also identify different bearers for a purported obligation.
The shared
record must distinguish an identified party from a framework's attribution
of responsibility to that party.

A reporting statute is a candidate rule referent.
Under $\RULEC$, the
comparison asks whether specified normative content is preserved; under
$\RULES$, it asks whether a specified textual or internal structure is
preserved.
Re-codification might preserve requirements while changing
section organization, yielding different \emph{proposed} classifications.
That comparison does not decide whether the resulting enactment is the
same statute as a legal object.
Its legal continuity, the meaning of its
requirements, and the intended unit of reference must be justified
independently.

Filings, inspections, transactions, and enforcement actions provide
candidate occurrences for $\OCC$.
A filing occurrence is distinguishable
from the filed document and from subsequent copies or versions of that
document, which can be examined under $\REC$.
Two jurisdictions may agree
which filing occurred while disagreeing whether it constituted compliance.
They may also disagree about whether two reported activities are the same
occurrence.
In the latter case, common occurrence identity cannot simply
be assumed from matching timestamps or document identifiers.

A jurisdictional scope can be examined by the cases it covers
($\SCOPEE$) or by a specified administrative or procedural structure
($\SCOPES$).
Dividing a reporting domain into districts may preserve
its coverage while changing its partition.
This supplies a conditional
transformation comparison, not a general rule that the jurisdiction
itself persists under subdivision.
The identity of a legal jurisdiction
may depend on further institutional facts not captured by either profile.

A regulated facility, parcel, or instrument supplies a possible plain
referent.
The $\LOC$ comparison follows a site or location, whereas
$\OBJ$ follows the object occupying or moving through it.
A facility
relocated from one site to another need not cease to be the same
facility; conversely, continued use of one site does not make successive
occupants one object.
The proposed basis must match the intended bearer.

These comparisons make all selected carrier kinds available for
analysis and illustrate the candidate split pairs.
A particular system may need only some of the candidate profiles.
Agreement about a carrier's designation does not by itself supply the
identity criterion needed to compare it across jurisdictions.

\subsection{Legislative History Tracking}
\label{subsec:app_legislative}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

A statute can be tracked by its recorded requirements, its enacted text,
its section structure, or the legal continuity of the instrument across
amendments.
The selected $\RULEC$ and $\RULES$ profiles isolate content-
and structure-based comparisons.
An amendment may retain specified
requirements while reorganizing sections; a textual continuation may
also be assigned a changed interpretation.
Neither observation alone
establishes the strict identity of the statute before and after the
amendment.
The referent and its identity conditions must first be fixed.

Enactments, votes, amendments, applications, and judicial decisions are
candidate occurrences for $\OCC$.
An enactment is not the enacted rule,
and an account of an enactment is not the enactment itself.
Legislative
texts, committee reports, docket entries, and interpretive memoranda
supply record candidates for $\REC$.
Copies with identical content may
be distinct documentary tokens, and later versions may differ without
settling whether they remain the same informational object.
The record
basis must therefore be specified rather than presumed.

Legislatures, agencies, courts, officers, and regulated parties can be
considered as obligation- or authority-bearing parties under $\OBL$,
provided the intended bearer is distinguished from an office, its
occupant, or a particular institutional role.
The same office may have
successive occupants; the same person may move to another office.
Such changes do not, by themselves, settle the identity of either the
person or the office.

Geographic, procedural, temporal, and institutional boundaries provide
candidate scopes.
A redistricting operation can preserve a chosen set
of covered cases while changing a partition, offering a comparison
between $\SCOPEE$ and $\SCOPES$.
Objects affected by the legal regime,
such as land parcels, facilities, or instruments, may be considered
under $\LOC$ or $\OBJ$ when those bases are appropriate.

Legislative history therefore offers tests for whether distinctions
among an instrument, its events, its documents, its institutional
bearers, and its scopes can be maintained without adopting contested
normative conclusions.
The formal profile catalog organizes these
tests; it does not decide which legal theory of continuity is correct.

\subsection{Cross-Institutional Provenance}
\label{subsec:app_provenance}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cross-institutional provenance highlights the distinction between a
record and what the record purports to describe.
A dataset entry,
measurement report, transfer log, annotation, or derived metric is a
candidate for $\REC$.
A copy may preserve content while creating a
new token; an annotation or derivation may instead create a new version
or informational object.
Neither common content nor an unbroken
provenance chain alone settles which record-identity criterion applies.

A sampled object, instrument, facility, or collection site is a
candidate plain referent.
The $\LOC$ profile asks about continuity of
a specified site or position; the $\OBJ$ profile asks about continuity
of a specified object.
Forking record representations does not
necessarily fork the represented object.
Conversely, a process that
separates, replaces, consumes, or transforms physical material may
raise identity questions that preservation of a location or inventory
label cannot resolve.
The intended referent and transformation effects
must be established separately.

Producing organizations, custodians, laboratories, agencies, and
recipients supply possible $\OBL$ carriers where their continuity and
responsibility-bearing status are independently specified.
Transfers,
measurements, analyses, approvals, and custody changes supply possible
$\OCC$ carriers.
A transfer log is a record of a proposed transfer
occurrence, not automatically that occurrence or proof of its identity.

Data-use policies, consent terms, retention rules, and access
restrictions provide candidate rule referents for $\RULEC$ or
$\RULES$.
Institutional coverage conditions, consent domains, and
data-sharing boundaries provide candidate scopes for $\SCOPEE$ or
$\SCOPES$.
Whether changes to policy text, permitted uses, scope
coverage, or organizational structure preserve the relevant referent
remains dependent on justified identity criteria.

These distinctions help separate shared reference from disagreements
about data quality, classification, causation, responsibility, or
permissible use.
They also expose circumstances in which institutions
lack enough warranted common reference for the Neutral Substrate
assumption to be discharged.
In that case, disputed co-reference or
persistence judgments should not be treated as established merely
because the records can be joined.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:app_summary}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The settings illustrate the analytic use of the selected
carrier--basis catalog: $\OBL$, $\OCC$, and $\REC$ organize questions
about bearers, occurrences, and records; $\LOC/\OBJ$,
$\SCOPEE/\SCOPES$, and $\RULEC/\RULES$ organize candidate comparisons
between different bases for plain referents, scopes, and rules.
Their usefulness lies in keeping these questions explicit.
The examples do not independently validate the table's transformation
classifications.
To assess scientific adequacy, a concrete study must
identify the intended referents and eligible presentations, justify
its proposed identity criteria, and evaluate the resulting judgments
against relevant examples and counterexamples.

Reference kinds select questions for analysis; declared bases propose
criteria; transformation profiles state candidate consequences.
Strict identity and persistence under transformation require further
warrant, and the conditions for combining such criteria without loss
remain open.
