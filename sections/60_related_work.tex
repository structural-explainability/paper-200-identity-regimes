\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =============================================================================

This paper is situated at the intersection of formal ontology, provenance,
and classification under disagreement.
It does not propose a new upper
ontology or derive a universally required inventory of identity regimes.
Instead, it compares selected carrier--basis profiles under specified
transformations and identifies what must still be established before
those profiles can support warranted identity judgments.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Classification under Disagreement}
\label{subsec:related_classification_disagreement}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Classification systems can embed institutional, social, and evaluative
commitments rather than functioning as neutral descriptions~\citep{bowkerstar1999,haslanger2012}.
In scientific and policy settings, disagreement may persist because
participants adopt different evidentiary, methodological, or evaluative
frameworks, not merely because a temporary empirical uncertainty remains~\citep{longino1990}.
These observations motivate shared representations that do not presuppose
interpretive convergence.

The present paper takes this motivation from \emph{Neutral Substrates}~\citep{case2026neutral}.
That work establishes a conditional sufficiency result for semantic
neutrality under Referential Common Ground; it does not supply
independently justified identity criteria for every intended referent.
Here the narrower question is how proposed identity bases respond to
specified record transformations, and what evidence would justify their
use for shared reference.
The selected nine-profile catalog illustrates
possible distinctions, not an irreducible minimum imposed by disagreement.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Identity, Roles, and Ontological Methodology}
\label{subsec:related_identity_roles}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Formal ontology distinguishes identity criteria from ordinary
classification and emphasizes the need to examine how roles and other
properties relate to the identity of their bearers.
OntoClean, in particular, analyzes rigidity and identity-related
constraints on taxonomic organization~\citep{guarino1998roles,guarinowelty2002}.
Accounts of social and institutional entities further examine bearers,
roles, social objects, and institutional structures~\citep{masolo2003wonderweb,guizzardi2005}.

This paper uses those distinctions as motivation, not as a derivation
of its proposed identity bases.
A role can be relevant to representing
or tracking a referent without independently supplying a sufficient
criterion for that referent's strict identity.
Conversely, the use of
an explicit role or basis parameter does not by itself make a
representation inadequate.
The question is which referent is intended,
what conditions justify treating presentations as the same referent,
and what a transformation actually preserves.

The paper compares declared transformation classifications under
candidate identity bases.
A difference between classifications is a
formal distinction, but it does not alone prove a difference between
generated equivalence relations or scientifically warranted identities.

\subsection{Upper Ontologies}
\label{subsec:related_upper_ontologies}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Upper ontologies such as BFO, DOLCE, and UFO provide resources for
modeling entities, occurrences, dependence, qualities, roles, and
institutional or social objects~\citep{smithceusters2015bfo,arp2015building,gangemi2002dolce,guizzardi2005}.
They address broader ontology-engineering purposes than the finite
comparison undertaken here.
This paper neither claims that those
ontologies lack the necessary distinctions nor establishes that its
selected nine profiles are required additions to them.

For example, a BFO-based model might represent a particular occurrence
using an occurrent category and a material object using a continuant
category, subject to the ontology's actual modeling commitments.
Rules, records, and institutional bearers likewise require analysis of
the intended referents and the resources available in a chosen ontology.
Such mappings cannot be assumed solely from names in the selected
inventory.
They must be justified in the application concerned.

DOLCE- and UFO-based representations similarly offer resources for
modeling social objects, roles, qualities, and dependence relations.
A realization built with any of these frameworks can be examined for
whether it distinguishes, where appropriate, a rule's content from its
structure, a scope's coverage from its organization, a location from
an occupying object, and a record from the thing recorded.
Whether
those are identity distinctions, rather than differences of description
or representation, remains dependent on the selected referent and
independently justified identity criterion.

Sections~\ref{sec:algebra}--\ref{sec:bound} compare such candidate
distinctions and examine the limits of counting regimes.
No result
here demonstrates that an upper ontology must implement nine
irreducible regimes.
The proposed comparison can instead help make
modeling assumptions explicit and expose cases needing further
scientific analysis.

\subsection{Provenance, Versioning, and Exchange}
\label{subsec:related_provenance_exchange}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The transformation families used here resemble operations encountered
in provenance and versioning systems.
PROV provides vocabulary for
activities, entities, derivation, attribution, generation, use, and
revision~\citep{provdm2013}.
These relations may supply evidence about
how representations were produced or changed.
They do not, without
additional identity conditions, settle whether two records are the
same record, copies of a record, or records about the same referent.

An exchange formalism such as Common Logic may provide a setting for
expressing some of the relevant commitments~\citep{isoiec24707}.
Conservative extension and heterogeneous ontology exchange are
related logical concerns~\citep{hodges1997,kutz2010}.
However,
compatibility of a substrate with admissible frameworks is not, by
itself, a proof of conservativity, and this paper constructs neither
a Common Logic encoding nor a conservative-extension theorem.
Its formal results concern the selected transformation profiles and
the limits of conclusions drawn from them.

Related questions about long-lived reference and reuse arise in
information infrastructures and digital-twin systems~\citep{edwards2011,grieves2016}.
The candidate profiles offer questions to test in such settings:
what is the intended bearer, which transformations preserve it,
and what available evidence warrants those judgments?
The paper does not establish a generally applicable solution for
those systems.

\subsection{Causal and Normative Separation}
\label{subsec:related_causal_normative}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The distinction between observations or records and causal claims is
important in causal analysis: causal conclusions depend on modeling
assumptions beyond a descriptive record alone~\citep{pearl2009}.
The neutral-substrate constraint makes an analogous distinction at
the foundational layer.
A substrate may record that a specified
source asserted a causal or normative proposition without itself
adopting that proposition as fact.
Under the assumptions of
\emph{Neutral Substrates}, the permitted foundational restriction is
sufficient for semantic neutrality~\citep{case2026neutral}.

The present paper examines a different issue: whether identity
judgments about records, rules, occurrences, and their referents
remain justified across transformations.
Annotation, copying,
refinement, and provenance extension may preserve some features and
change others.
Which changes preserve a particular referent's strict
identity is not determined merely by a transformation-family label.
Keeping interpretation attributed also does not establish that the
referential commitments themselves are adequately grounded.

\subsection{FAIR Reuse under Persistent Disagreement}
\label{subsec:related_fair}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The FAIR principles motivate discoverable, accessible, interoperable,
and reusable data across communities and over time~\citep{wilkinson2016}.
They do not, by themselves, establish the referent-specific criteria
needed to justify every co-reference or persistence judgment under
persistent disagreement.
Shared vocabularies, identifiers, schemas,
and documented provenance can support reuse, but their presence alone
does not prove strict identity.

The finite construction studied here complements these concerns by
making selected identity-basis choices and proposed transformation
effects explicit.
Its nine candidate profiles can serve as comparison
cases where different identity questions arise during reuse.
Whether
a particular case calls for separate profiles, an explicit
carrier-indexed representation, or another adequate construction
requires independent analysis.
No general nine-regime requirement
follows from FAIR reuse or from the present catalog.
